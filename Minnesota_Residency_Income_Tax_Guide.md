# Minnesota Residency and Income Tax Guide

## Overview
This guide explains how to establish your Minnesota residency date for tax purposes and how to properly report income earned before and after you moved to Minnesota.

---

## Key Point: No Single "Residency Establishment Form"

Minnesota does not have a single state tax form that you file to declare when your residency started. Instead, you establish residency through your actions (moving, obtaining a driver's license, etc.) and then document it through your tax return filing.

---

## Tax Forms You Need to File

### Form M1 + Schedule M1NR (Filed Together)

**You file BOTH together as a package.**

- **Form M1** = Your main Minnesota Individual Income Tax Return
- **Schedule M1NR** = Supplemental schedule that goes WITH Form M1
  - This is where you document your residency dates
  - This shows how your income is split between resident and nonresident portions

You cannot file M1 without including Schedule M1NR if you are a part-year resident.

---

## Part-Year Resident Status

If you moved to Minnesota during a tax year and planned to stay permanently, you are considered a **part-year resident** regardless of how many days you spent in Minnesota that year.

### Filing Requirements
- You must file a Minnesota return if your Minnesota gross income meets the minimum filing requirement ($14,950 for 2025)
- File Form M1 and Schedule M1NR together

---

## How Your Income Gets Split

The key to avoiding taxation on pre-move income is properly documenting WHEN your residency started.

### Step 1: Calculate Income While a Minnesota Resident
Include income from ALL sources (no matter where earned):
- Wages, salary, bonuses
- Interest, dividends, capital gains
- Business income
- Out-of-state income earned while a Minnesota resident

### Step 2: Calculate Minnesota-Source Income While a Nonresident
Only income earned from Minnesota sources is taxable:
- Wages/salaries for work performed in Minnesota
- Minnesota business income
- Minnesota property sales
- Minnesota-sourced gains and interest

### Step 3: Combine Totals
If total Minnesota gross income meets the $14,950 threshold, you must file.

---

## Tax Treatment Based on Your Status

### For the Portion of the Year You Were a Minnesota Resident
- You pay Minnesota tax on **ALL income from all sources**
- This includes out-of-state income

### For the Portion of the Year You Were a Nonresident
- You pay Minnesota tax **ONLY on income derived from Minnesota sources**
- Out-of-state income earned while nonresident is not taxed by Minnesota

---

## How to Establish Your Residency Date

Minnesota recognizes two main tests for establishing residency:

### Test 1: Domicile (Permanent Residency)
- You moved to Minnesota during the tax year and established residency
- Your "domicile" is the place you intend to make your home permanently or indefinitely
- Once established, it continues until you take action to change it

### Test 2: The 183-Day Rule
- You spent at least 183 days in Minnesota during the year
- You rented, owned, occupied, or maintained an "abode" (a residence suitable for year-round use with cooking and bathing facilities)
- Any part of a day counts as a full day
- Exceptions: certain travel days and days spent in Minnesota for medical treatment may not count

---

## Documentation to Keep

You don't file these with the state, but keep them for your records in case of an audit:

### Proof of Residency Date
- Lease agreement or property deed (dated with move-in date)
- Minnesota driver's license application date
- Vehicle registration date in Minnesota
- First day of employment in Minnesota
- Utility bills showing move-in date
- Moving company records or receipts

### Records to Support Your Residency Status
Minnesota aggressively audits residency claims and may review:
- Travel documents and plane tickets
- Calendars and planners
- Credit card and bank statements
- Utility bills
- Cell phone location data
- Social media activity

Keep copies of documents that establish your presence and ties to Minnesota.

---

## Common Audit Triggers

Be especially careful with documentation if:
- You maintained Minnesota real estate after moving
- You kept professional licenses in Minnesota
- You left a spouse or dependents in Minnesota
- You continued earning significant Minnesota income
- You claimed to be a nonresident but had strong Minnesota ties

---

## Income Allocation Tips

### For Earned Income (Wages, Salary, Bonuses)
- Use your actual move date to split income
- Example: If you moved on July 15, allocate income earned before July 15 to the nonresident period and after July 15 to the resident period
- Your payroll department can help with accurate allocation using timesheets

### For Unearned Income (Dividends, Interest, Capital Gains)
- Allocate based on the state you were a resident of when you received the income
- Example: If you received dividend payments before your move date, allocate to the nonresident period
- If you sold stock after moving to Minnesota, allocate the gain to the resident period

---

## Home Sales and Capital Gains

Minnesota does tax capital gains from home sales. However:

### Primary Residence Exclusion
- Single filers: Up to **$250,000** of capital gains can be excluded
- Married couples filing jointly: Up to **$500,000** of capital gains can be excluded
- You must have owned and used the home as your principal residence for at least 2 of the last 5 years

**Most homeowners don't owe capital gains tax on home sales** because the profit is below these limits.

If you sell a home after becoming a Minnesota resident, the capital gains would be subject to Minnesota tax (unless covered by the exclusion).

## Summary of Key Points

1. ✓ **File both Form M1 AND Schedule M1NR together**
2. ✓ **Document your exact residency start date** with lease agreements, driver's license, employment dates, etc.
3. ✓ **Split your income** based on what portion of the year you were a resident vs. nonresident
4. ✓ **For resident period**: All income from all sources is taxable
5. ✓ **For nonresident period**: Only Minnesota-source income is taxable
6. ✓ **Keep records** of your move date and income allocation for potential audits
